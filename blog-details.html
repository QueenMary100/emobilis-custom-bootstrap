How to Brew Great Pour-Over at Home (Without Fancy Gear)

Wambui Njeri

Founder & Head Roaster
Oct 9, 2026

You do not need a barista course or an expensive grinder to make a cup that beats most cafés. You need a scale, hot water and about four minutes.

What you need

  • A pour-over dripper (a V60-style cone or flat-bottom basket) and paper filters
  • A kitchen scale that reads in grams
  • A kettle, ideally with a narrow spout, and a timer
  • 20 g of fresh coffee, ground medium-fine, about the texture of table salt

The 1:16 recipe

  • 320 g of water for 20 g of coffee
  • Water just off the boil, about 94 °C (wait 30 seconds after boiling)
  • Total brew time of 3 to 3.5 minutes

Step by step

  1. Rinse the paper filter with hot water and discard the water.
  2. Add the coffee, level it and zero the scale.
  3. Pour 40 g of water and wait 40 seconds. This is the bloom, when trapped gas escapes.
  4. Pour slowly in circles until the scale reads 320 g, aiming to finish by 2:00.
  5. Let it drain, remove the dripper, swirl the cup and taste.

“If it tastes sour, grind finer. If it tastes bitter, grind coarser. Change one thing at a time.”

Wambui Njeri, Head Roaster

Start with our Ridge House Blend for a forgiving, chocolatey cup, or the Nyeri AA when you want the fruit to shine. Brew it within a few weeks of the roast date and it will taste like the bag promised.

Why Kenyan Coffee Tastes Like Blackcurrant

Kamau Gitau

Farmer Relations
Sep 18, 2026

Ask ten people to describe a good Kenyan coffee and at least three will say blackcurrant. It is not marketing. It is geography.

Altitude and volcanic soil

Most of Kenya's best coffee grows between 1,400 and 2,100 metres on the slopes of Mt Kenya and the Aberdare range. Cool nights slow the ripening of the cherry, so it builds more sugars and acids. The red volcanic soil is rich in minerals and drains well.

Altitude

1,400 to 2,100 metres; cool nights, slow ripening, dense beans.

Varieties

SL28 and SL34, selected in Kenya in the 1930s for flavour and drought resistance.

Washed with care

At the cooperative's wet mill, ripe cherries are pulped, fermented overnight, graded in water channels, soaked and then dried on raised beds for one to two weeks. Each step removes defects and sharpens the acidity people love.

“A farmer can do everything right for a year and lose it in a day on the drying bed. That is why we visit.”

Kamau Gitau, Farmer Relations

Kenya has two harvests each year: a main crop late in the year and a smaller fly crop mid-year. Our Nyeri AA comes from the main crop, when the cherries ripen slowly and the cups are at their sweetest.

How to Read a Kenyan Coffee Bag: AA, AB and PB Explained

Achieng Otieno

Quality & Cupping Lead
Aug 21, 2026

AA, AB, PB: the letters on a Kenyan coffee bag look like exam results. They are not. Here is what they actually tell you.

Grades are about size

Kenyan green coffee is sorted by bean size and density. AA is the largest (screen size 17/18), AB is slightly smaller (screen size 15/16), and PB, or peaberry, is a single round bean where the cherry produced one seed instead of two.

Quick guide

  • AA: large, dense beans, often bright and complex
  • AB: medium beans, usually balanced and sweet
  • PB: small and round, roasts evenly and is often juicy

Size is not quality

A big bean can taste flat and a small one can taste spectacular. Flavour comes from the farm, the variety, how the cherry was processed and how fresh the roast is. That is why we cup every lot before we buy it.

What else to look for

  • The roast date, not just a best-before date
  • The name of the cooperative or farm
  • The process, such as washed or natural
  • The altitude and harvest year

A good bag tells you where, when and how. If yours only says “Kenya AA”, ask for more.

A Day on the Roast Floor: From Green Bean to Your Cup

Wambui Njeri

Founder & Head Roaster
Jul 3, 2026

Every Monday and Thursday at 6 am the roastery smells like toast, cocoa and warm hay. This is what happens between a sack of green beans and the bag on your doorstep.

Roasting in twelve minutes

Green beans go into the drum at roughly 200 °C. For the first few minutes they dry and turn from green to yellow. Around the middle of the roast they brown and their sugars begin to caramelise. When the beans reach about 196 °C they crack with a sound like popcorn. That is first crack, and we drop the batch shortly after, depending on the flavour we are chasing. A 12 kg batch takes about twelve minutes.

Our roast-day timeline

  • 6:00 Warm up the roaster and cup yesterday's batch
  • 7:00 First batch in the drum
  • Next morning: rest, pack and label
  • Courier pick-up and rider deliveries

Resting and cupping

Fresh-roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide for days. We let every batch rest overnight, cup a sample against the last batch, and only then pack it. Filter coffee is delicious within a few days; espresso blends often taste best from day four or five.

Want to see it for yourself? Come to the roastery on a Saturday morning and watch a roast from the first crack to the cooling tray.
